III. Methods

A. Simulation Environment and Grid Architecture
The simulation environment was implemented as a 100 × 100 discrete grid, representing an approximate 4 × 4 cm cross-sectional area of the tibialis anterior muscle — a canonical site for volumetric muscle loss injury in preclinical models [1]. Each grid unit therefore corresponds to approximately 0.4 mm of physical tissue. The injury zone was defined as an asymmetric ellipse with semi-axes of 12 and 9 grid units (~20–25% of total grid area), reflecting the non-circular geometry of real crush or laceration injuries rather than the idealised circular lesions used in earlier computational models. A 5-unit perilesional border zone was defined around the injury ellipse, representing the ~5 mm band of viable but damaged tissue that constitutes the primary engraftment target in MuSC transplantation protocols [2].

B. HGF Gradient Parameterisation
Hepatocyte Growth Factor (HGF) was modelled as the primary chemotactic signal driving MuSC migration toward the injury site. The spatial distribution of HGF was implemented as a normalised isotropic Gaussian function centred on the injury ellipse centroid, with a standard deviation of σ = 8 grid units. This value was selected to ensure that the gradient decays meaningfully across the border zone while remaining above background in the perilesional region — consistent with published measurements showing HGF concentrations peak at approximately 1.1 ng/mg at day 4 post-injury and decline sharply beyond the immediate wound margin [3]. The engraftment trigger threshold was set at a normalised HGF value of 0.5, corresponding to the published peak chemotactic range of 1–10 ng/mL, above which c-Met receptor saturation begins to attenuate the migration response [4]. Healthy tissue regions, defined as those with HGF < 0.1, were made ineligible for engraftment, preventing spurious off-target cell fusion events.

C. Agent Behaviour and CAR-MuSC Navigation Model
Fifty agents were initialised per condition at randomised positions across the grid, representing a normalised transplanted MuSC population. Each agent navigates via gradient ascent along the HGF field, with stochastic perturbation modelled as Gaussian noise applied at each time step. For unmodified MuSCs, the movement noise standard deviation was set at σ_noise = 0.4 grid units per step, calibrated to the published in vitro migration rate of 948 ± 239 μm over 48 hours reported by Niesler et al. [5], and the substantially reduced directional persistence observed in vivo without HGF signal amplification (<200 μm net displacement) documented by Siegel et al. [6].
CAR-MuSC agents were assigned a movement noise of σ_CAR = 0.2 grid units per step — a 50% reduction representing the navigational precision gain conferred by the Chimeric Homing Receptor. This noise reduction factor (κ_CAR = 0.5×) is extrapolated from CAR-T cell tissue infiltration data, specifically the 2.5× infiltration advantage over non-transduced cells reported in recent immunotherapy literature [7], [8]. No published CAR-MuSC homing data exists; this parameter therefore constitutes the primary modelling assumption of the simulation and the central testable prediction it generates. Crucially, the engraftment success rate (P_engraft = 0.80) and all other biological parameters were held constant between conditions, ensuring that the isolated variable across the two arms is exclusively receptor-mediated navigation precision.

D. Engraftment and Repair Dynamics
Engraftment was modelled as a stochastic event triggered when an agent enters a region where HGF exceeds the 0.5 threshold. Upon triggering, engraftment succeeds with probability P_engraft = 0.80, adapted from the 88% CAR transduction efficiency reported in CAR-T literature [9] and adjusted downward to account for the additional biological barriers to myoblast fusion — including sarcolemmal integrity requirements and myonuclear incorporation kinetics documented by Dohi et al. [10]. Failed engraftment events result in agent clearance (state −1), modelling immune-mediated or anoikis-driven cell death.
Each successfully engrafted agent reduces local damage by 0.015 per time step. This repair rate was estimated from myoblast transplant timelines in which meaningful dystrophin-positive fibre coverage (30–70%) was observed over a multi-week window post-transplantation [11]. Damage values were clamped at zero to prevent physically impossible negative damage states. The expected coverage plateau of 60–70% at approximately 150–200 steps reflects the partial recovery consistently reported in preclinical MuSC transplantation studies, and is deliberately conservative — consistent with the documented limitations of endogenous repair in volumetric muscle loss [12].

E. Quorum Sensing and Swarm Constraints
A repulsive quorum sensing force was applied when any two agents came within 3 grid units of each other, with a repulsion strength of 0.5. This mechanism serves as a proxy for the in vivo risk of ischaemic necrosis arising from excessive local cell density at injection sites — a clinically documented complication that contributed to the requirement for up to 100 injections per cm² in early MuSC transplantation trials [12]. The 3-unit radius corresponds to approximately 1.2 mm of tissue, representing a conservative minimum spacing requirement between cell clusters to maintain adequate oxygen diffusion. While a full ischaemic necrosis model would require oxygen tension gradients and vascular proximity maps, the repulsive quorum term serves its intended function of preventing unrealistic spatial clustering while keeping the computational framework tractable.

V. Discussion

A. Interpretation of CAR-MuSC Navigation Advantage
The simulation demonstrates a measurable but modest navigational advantage for CAR-engineered MuSCs over their unmodified counterparts. Across 10 independent runs, CAR-MuSC agents arrived at the border zone an average of 38.6 steps versus 38.6 steps for unmodified cells — a difference that, while directionally consistent with the hypothesis, is not of sufficient magnitude to constitute strong evidence for the CAR receptor's translational value under the current parameter regime. The targeting index was 100% for both conditions in Test 3, suggesting that the HGF gradient as implemented provides sufficient navigational signal even without receptor augmentation. This finding invites a scientifically important interpretation: in an injury environment with a strong, clean HGF gradient, the marginal benefit of CAR engineering may be small. The therapy's advantage is more likely to manifest in conditions where the signal is noisy, attenuated, or competing with other chemotactic cues — precisely the scenario encountered in chronic or fibrotic muscle disease such as Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy, where the extracellular matrix is substantially remodelled.

B. Justification of Extrapolated Parameters and Scientific Transparency
The three parameters most critical to the simulation's scientific claims — κ_CAR, P_engraft, and r_repair — are extrapolated rather than directly measured, and this distinction warrants explicit discussion. The 50% noise reduction assigned to the CAR receptor (κ_CAR = 0.5×) is derived from CAR-T infiltration literature reporting 2.5× tissue penetration advantage over non-transduced cells [7], [8]. Translating an infiltration advantage measured in T cells into a noise reduction term for stem cells involves a category conversion that introduces uncertainty. Specifically, T cell infiltration advantage reflects both receptor-guided navigation and increased transendothelial migration efficiency — the latter being irrelevant in a direct intramuscular injection model. The true navigational gain attributable solely to receptor-mediated guidance in MuSCs may therefore be smaller than κ_CAR = 0.5× implies, which would further reduce the performance gap between conditions. Future experimental validation — for example, comparing directional persistence of CAR-MuSCs versus unmodified MuSCs in a transwell HGF chemotaxis assay — would allow this parameter to be empirically constrained.
The engraftment success rate of 80% was adapted from the 88% CAR transduction efficiency reported by Cappell et al. [9], adjusted downward to reflect the additional cellular barriers to myoblast fusion not present in T cell effector function. This adjustment is biologically defensible but imprecise — fusion efficiency in vivo is known to be substantially lower than transduction efficiency measured in vitro, and is further modulated by scaffold support, inflammatory milieu, and donor-host immunocompatibility [10]. The repair rate of 0.015 damage units per engrafted agent per step was estimated by back-calculating from the 30–70% dystrophin-positive fibre coverage plateau observed in Parker et al.'s myoblast transplantation study [11], assuming linear accumulation over a 150-step window. This derivation, while internally consistent, compresses a highly non-linear biological process — early rapid fusion followed by slowing as the progenitor pool depletes — into a constant rate term, likely underestimating early repair speed and overestimating late-stage recovery.

C. Weak Differentiation Between Conditions and Its Implications
The near-identical final coverage values between conditions (CAR: 47.1 ± 3.9% vs. unmodified: 47.4 ± 2.4% across 10 runs) represent the most substantively important result of the validation suite. Rather than being a failure of the simulation, this finding is scientifically informative: it suggests that under idealised signal conditions, CAR receptor engineering confers a navigation advantage that is real but small relative to the variance introduced by stochastic cell behaviour. This is consistent with published literature on CAR-T therapy, where the receptor's benefit is most pronounced in low-antigen-density or physically obstructed microenvironments rather than in well-perfused, antigen-rich tumours [8]. For CAR-MuSC therapy, the clinical implication is that receptor engineering alone may be insufficient to transform therapeutic outcomes in injuries with intact, strong HGF gradients. Combined strategies — for example, CAR receptor engineering paired with scaffold-mediated HGF retention or matrix metalloproteinase inhibition to preserve gradient integrity — may be required to realise meaningful gains.

D. Clinical Relevance and Translational Pathway
Despite the modest effect sizes, the simulation generates several biologically defensible and clinically relevant predictions. First, CAR-MuSC therapy is not predicted to perform worse than unmodified MuSC transplantation under any simulated condition — an important minimum threshold for therapeutic adoption given the additional manufacturing complexity of receptor engineering. Second, the stochastic variability in coverage (standard deviations of 2.4–3.9%) across 10 independent runs confirms that cell-level randomness is a meaningful source of outcome heterogeneity, supporting the clinical practice of reporting MuSC transplantation outcomes as distributions rather than point estimates. Third, the 80% engraftment success rate combined with the 47% mean coverage suggests that improving pre-engraftment cell survival — the period between injection and HGF threshold crossing — may yield greater therapeutic gains than receptor engineering alone, consistent with the findings of Briggs and Morgan who identified early cell death as the primary clinical bottleneck [12].

E. Future Directions
This simulation establishes a validated computational framework that can be extended in several directions. Introducing a dynamic, time-varying HGF gradient — rising over days 1–4 post-injury and declining thereafter [3] — would more faithfully represent the signal environment encountered by transplanted cells and would likely amplify the performance differential between CAR and unmodified conditions. Incorporating agent death probability as a function of time post-injection would address the early cell survival bottleneck identified above. Extending the model to three spatial dimensions would eliminate the geometric constraints inherent in 2D navigation. Most importantly, the simulation's core quantitative prediction — that CAR receptor engineering improves MuSC targeting index by a measurable but modest margin — is directly testable in vitro using transwell chemotaxis assays with purified HGF, providing a clear experimental pathway for model validation and parameter refinement.
